In just over a year, Nepal went from deadly street protests to the biggest single-party election win in decades and the youngest prime minister in its recent history. This explainer sets out what happened, in order, with links to the reports behind each fact.

## Timeline at a glance

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| Early Sept 2025 | The government blocks 26 social media platforms that had not registered with the authorities |
| 8 Sept 2025 | Youth-led "Gen Z" protests begin; police respond with deadly force |
| 9 Sept 2025 | Prime Minister K. P. Sharma Oli resigns |
| 12 Sept 2025 | Parliament is dissolved; former Chief Justice **Sushila Karki** becomes interim prime minister, the first woman to lead a government in Nepal |
| 5 Mar 2026 | General election for all 275 seats of the House of Representatives |
| Mid-Mar 2026 | Final results: the **Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP)** wins 182 seats |
| 27 Mar 2026 (Chaitra 13, 2082) | **Balendra "Balen" Shah** is sworn in as prime minister |

## How it started: the Gen Z protests

The protests began on 8 September 2025, after the government blocked 26 social media platforms. They quickly grew into a wider revolt against corruption and the old political leadership, driven largely by young people. The unrest left **at least 76 people dead** and thousands injured, according to figures reported by Reuters and summarised on [Wikipedia's election article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Nepalese_general_election). Prime Minister Oli resigned on 9 September.

On 12 September, President Ram Chandra Paudel dissolved the House of Representatives and appointed **Sushila Karki**, a former Chief Justice, as interim prime minister, with elections set for 5 March 2026.

## The March 2026 election

About **59% of registered voters** took part. The result was a landslide for the RSP, which had chosen former Kathmandu mayor Balen Shah as its prime ministerial candidate:

| Party | Seats (of 275) |
|---|---|
| Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP) | **182** |
| Nepali Congress | 38 |
| CPN (UML) | 25 |
| Nepali Communist Party | 17 |
| Shram Sanskriti Party | 7 |
| Rastriya Prajatantra Party | 5 |

A majority needs 138 seats. The RSP's result was the first time since 1999 that a single party won a majority on its own, and its 47.8% share of the proportional vote was the highest since that system began in 2008. Both Nepali Congress and CPN (UML) had their worst results ever ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Nepalese_general_election), citing Reuters, Al Jazeera and The Guardian).

In the most watched race, Shah stood against Oli himself in **Jhapa-5**, and won. The four-time prime minister lost his seat ([The Hitavada](https://www.thehitavada.com/Encyc/2026/3/8/balendra-shah-defeats-4-time-pm-oli-rsp-set-to-form-next-govt-in-nepal.html)).

## Prime Minister Balen Shah

Shah, a structural engineer and former rapper who became Kathmandu's mayor as an independent in 2022, was sworn in on **27 March 2026** by President Paudel under Article 76(1) of the Constitution. At 35, he is described as Nepal's youngest prime minister in decades and the first from the **Madhesh** region ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/asia/south-asia/article/3348119/balendra-shah-sworn-nepals-prime-minister); [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/international/nepal-swears-in-balendra-shah-as-youngest-prime-minister)). Harvard-educated economist **Swarnim Waglé** became finance minister.

## The first months in power

The government launched a **100-point governance reform agenda** and cut the number of ministries ([Tribune India, one month in](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/world/marred-by-controversy-light-on-reforms-nepals-super-pm-balen-shah-completes-one-month-in-office/)). Early reviews were mixed:

- Supporters pointed to a fast pace and a "zero tolerance" line on corruption and procurement. The Public Procurement Monitoring Office says **120 firms have been blacklisted since 27 March** ([Nepal News economic brief, 30 Oct 2026](https://english.nepalnews.com/s/business/nepal-news-evening-economic-brief-october-30-2026/)).
- Critics pointed to two ministers resigning within the first month, a controversial demolition drive against riverbank settlements in the Kathmandu Valley, and a prime minister who rarely speaks to the press ([The Annapurna Express](https://theannapurnaexpress.com/story/63906/); [Gulf Times, 100 days](https://www.gulf-times.com/article/728679/international/nepal/nepal-pm-marks-100-days-with-fast-change-and-few-words)).
- The Kathmandu Post's opinion pages described the early steps as reform-minded, with credibility depending on implementation ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/opinion/2026/04/29/nepal-strives-for-provincial-improvement-will-their-lot-improve)).

## Where things stand now (late October 2026)

- **Press freedom:** after reports of police entering newsrooms and questioning journalists, the RSP itself directed the government to investigate, with spokesperson Jagadish Kharel saying the party would "not compromise" on press freedom ([Nepal News briefing, 31 Oct 2026](https://english.nepalnews.com/s/main-right/nepal-news-evening-briefing-saturday-october-31-2026/)).
- **The courts:** the RSP has discussed pursuing parliamentary impeachment as part of "judicial restructuring", and planned consultations with opposition parties, including Nepali Congress president Gagan Thapa (same source).
- **The economy:** public debt has passed **Rs 3 trillion**, and the UN has given Nepal until **24 November 2029** to graduate from Least Developed Country status. Both are covered in our [weekly round-up](/nepal-news-roundup-week-of-26-october-2026/).

## Why it matters

The 2026 result was a clear vote against the parties that had led Nepal for decades. A single-party majority means the RSP cannot blame coalition partners. The next year will show whether fast, top-down reform can turn into lasting change for jobs, prices and services, the issues that brought young people onto the streets.
